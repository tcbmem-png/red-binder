# Research — Detention Decision Form

_Internal memo. Drafted 2026-06-17. For the proposed Red Binder "what to do if I'm detained" function. Not legal advice to any client; an internal scoping and accuracy memo for TCB Law / Red Binder Project._

---

## The short version

You are right. The thing ICE is pushing in detention centers right now is a **stipulated order of removal**. It deports the person about as fast as voluntary departure would, but it leaves them with a **final order of removal** on their record. That order is the lingering damage. Voluntary departure gets to the same place — the person leaves the country in a few weeks — without the order.

So your instinct is correct: same effect on the quick-deport part, very different effect on the lingering part. But there are two truths you need to build around, because both are easy to get wrong:

1. **A form the person carries cannot grant voluntary departure or bond.** Only DHS or an immigration judge can. The wallet form's real job is to make the person refuse the stipulation, ask for a judge, and hand over a pre-made decision and contact list. It is a script and a record, not a grant of relief.

2. **Voluntary departure is not the clean win it's often sold as.** It avoids the removal order, but it does **not** erase the 3- and 10-year unlawful presence bars. For someone who has been here a year or more without status, VD's only real advantages over a removal order are (a) no criminal illegal-reentry exposure under 8 U.S.C. § 1326, and (b) no removal-order ground of inadmissibility. That matters most for people who have a real legal path back (a family petition). For someone with no path back, NIJC says plainly that VD is "little better than a removal order." The form should not oversell it.

## What ICE is actually getting people to sign

There are three different documents in play. They are not the same, and the danger is that they get blurred together on purpose.

### 1. Stipulated order of removal — the one to worry about

This is the form the National Immigration Project flagged in spring 2025. ICE started handing it to detained people — including people **already in immigration court proceedings** — often pre-filled, in English and Spanish. It is an agreement (a "stipulation") between the detained person and ICE that the person will be **deported without ever seeing an immigration judge**, under 8 C.F.R. § 1003.25(b).

What the person gives up by signing it, in the form's own words:

- The right to make the government prove they're removable.
- The right to a hearing where a judge tells them whether they qualify for any relief.
- The right to question witnesses, present evidence, and have the hearing recorded.
- The right to appeal.
- It expressly states the person does **not** wish to apply for asylum, withholding, or CAT protection, and has **no fear** of return. (Signing this can wreck a future asylum claim.)
- For green-card holders, it surrenders LPR status permanently.

And it spells out the consequences the signer accepts: a bar on returning for **5, 10, or 20 years, possibly forever**; and that returning without permission could mean **criminal prosecution and up to 20 years in prison** (that's the § 1326 illegal-reentry exposure).

The end result is a **final order of removal**. Same legal weight as if a judge had ordered them deported after a full hearing.

**The coercion pattern (documented by NIPNLG):** officers tell people that if they don't sign, they'll be locked up for a long time or wait many months for a hearing. The honest counter is: not signing means waiting for a hearing — which can take several weeks to a month-plus because of backlog — but **nobody is required to sign**, and anyone can ask a judge to decide if they're eligible for bond. Signing doesn't even guarantee speed: removal after signing still takes weeks to months. So the person trades away all their rights and often doesn't even get out faster.

### 2. Voluntary departure — the safer alternative, with caveats

Voluntary departure (INA § 240B) lets a person leave at their own expense **without a removal order**. Two flavors:

- **Pre-hearing VD** (§ 240B(a)): up to 120 days, fewer eligibility bars. Must be requested before/at the early stage, must waive other relief and appeal, concede removability, have a passport, depart at own expense.
- **Post-conclusion VD** (§ 240B(b)): up to 60 days, harder — requires 1 year physical presence, 5 years good moral character, a bond of at least $500, and proof of means to depart.

Either DHS or the immigration judge can grant it. **Critical:** when DHS "offers" VD informally, make sure what they're actually putting in front of the person is a VD agreement and **not** a stipulated removal order dressed up as one. NIJC explicitly warns DHS's practice is to "persuade or even coerce individuals to sign stipulated orders of removal, which are NOT the same as voluntary departure."

The honest limits, for the form's disclaimers:

- VD does **not** cure the 3/10-year unlawful presence bars (INA § 212(a)(9)(B)). Anyone with a year+ of unlawful presence still eats a 10-year bar.
- **Failure to depart on time is a trap.** A missed VD deadline auto-converts to a removal order, plus a $1,000–$5,000 fine and a 10-year bar on adjustment, cancellation, registry, and future VD. If counsel isn't confident the person can actually leave on time, VD can leave them worse off than a removal order.
- Since January 2025, detained people granted VD have sometimes waited **longer** in custody before actually being removed. VD is not a guaranteed fast exit.
- If detained, the person doesn't get released — they're flown out directly from custody.

There's also **withdrawal of application for admission** (INA § 235(a)(4)) for people charged as "arriving" noncitizens, who can't get pre-hearing VD. Same goal — leave without a removal order — and it doesn't require a passport.

### 3. Self-deportation / "CBP Home" — not the same thing

If a person just boards a plane or walks across the border on their own, that is self-deportation. It is **not** a grant of voluntary departure and gives none of VD's protection. Worth naming explicitly so people don't think leaving on their own equals a clean VD.

### Form numbers to keep straight
- **I-826** — "Notice of Rights and Request for Disposition." The rights notice given at arrest; has options including requesting a hearing vs. requesting return.
- **I-275** — "Withdrawal of Admission / Voluntary Departure."
- **Stipulated order of removal** — the § 1003.25(b) document above. No clean public form number; often pre-printed, 6 pages, titled "Stipulation."

## The decision tree the form should encode

The branch point is **fight or leave**, and the form should make the person decide *in advance, while calm and with counsel*, not in the room under pressure.

**If FIGHT (wants to stay / has a possible claim):**
- Sign nothing.
- Say: "I am exercising my right to remain silent. I want to speak to my attorney. I want to see an immigration judge."
- Ask the judge for a **bond hearing** (note: generally only one bond hearing absent changed circumstances — so it matters to have help filing).
- Preserve any asylum/fear claim — do **not** sign anything saying you have no fear of return.

**If LEAVE (wants to go, but protect the future):**
- Still sign nothing on the spot. Refuse the stipulated removal order.
- Ask specifically for **voluntary departure** (or withdrawal of application for admission, if charged as arriving), in front of a judge.
- Understand the conditions: own expense, passport, deadline — and that missing the deadline converts it to a removal order with penalties.
- Know it may not be faster than fighting, and won't cure unlawful-presence bars.

**The single rule under both branches:** *Do not sign anything labeled a stipulation, a removal order, or a waiver of rights without talking to a lawyer.* That one sentence is most of the protective value.

## What the wallet form can and can't be

It **can** be:
- A **decision record** — the person's pre-chosen path (fight vs. depart), made calmly, that family and counsel can act on.
- A **rights script** — the exact sentences to say and the forms to refuse, in EN/ES.
- A **contact + key-facts card** — A-number (if any), attorney, emergency contacts, country of citizenship, time in U.S., whether they fear return. This dovetails with the existing Red Binder contact/agent data you already collect.
- A **"do not sign" card** — short, laminated, the one-sentence rule plus the magic words.

It **cannot** be:
- A grant of voluntary departure or bond. Only DHS/the IJ can grant those. The form can only tell the person to *request* them.
- A substitute for counsel. It should drive toward a lawyer, not replace one.
- A guarantee of any timeline or outcome.

This fits the Red Binder model cleanly: it's another "make the next right action easier to find" artifact — memory, not transformation. The honest framing is that it preserves options and prevents the one irreversible mistake (signing the stipulation), rather than promising a good outcome.

## The second problem: getting a lawyer connected after detention (the G-28 catch-22)

You named the real bottleneck. The instrument that lets you act for a detained person — and even *confirm they're detained* — is **Form G-28** (Notice of Entry of Appearance). Without a G-28 on file, **ICE will not release any information about the person to you.** That's the catch: you need authority to get information, but the person who can grant it is locked up and incommunicado, and there's no quick way to get the signature back to you. This is a separate problem from the "don't sign the stipulation" decision, but it can live on the same piece of paper.

### How the G-28 actually breaks the logjam

- **G-28 = the consent key.** Signing it both authorizes you to represent the person before DHS (USCIS/ICE/CBP) *and* counts as their consent to disclose their records to you. It's what makes ICE talk to you.
- **It can be signed in advance.** The established practice — used by rapid-response networks — is to keep an **undated, pre-signed G-28 on file for every client**, exactly so detention doesn't create a delay. In some jurisdictions ICE has even accepted a G-28 signed only by the representative for a detained person. So a G-28 executed *before* arrest, held by the attorney and copied in the binder, is the clean fix.
- **Two forms, not one, for a full detained case.** G-28 covers ICE/ERO (information, custody, bond). To appear before the immigration judge you also file **EOIR-28** (and EOIR-27 for the BIA). For the *contact* problem you're describing, the G-28 is the one that matters — it's what unlocks ICE.
- **Locating the person — and why it fails for the fully undocumented.** ICE's **Online Detainee Locator System** (locator.ice.gov) searches on **A-number + country of birth**, or exact legal name + DOB + country of birth. Two problems hit our exact population hardest:
  - **No A-number.** A-numbers are assigned at a DHS encounter (apprehension or a prior application). Someone who has never been encountered **has no A-number to pre-record** — so families are forced onto the name/DOB search, which is the weaker path. (If they *do* have one — prior NTA, work permit, old application — capture it; it's gold.)
  - **Mangled names.** ODLS only matches the name **exactly as the officer keyed it in.** Compound Spanish surnames (apellido paterno + materno) get truncated, swapped, hyphenated, or misspelled constantly, so the "right" search often returns nothing. The card has to capture **every permutation**: full legal name, both surnames separately, likely misspellings, any name variants/aliases the person has used — plus **exact DOB and country of birth**, which the search requires.
  - Because location is this fragile, the card's identity block should also hold enough for a human to identify the person: a **photo**, height/distinguishing details, and the names of everyone who might be looking for them. Caveats: ODLS excludes minors and lags for people just moved from CBP custody.
  - **Upshot:** for the undocumented-with-no-prior-contact case, the locator is unreliable by design. The durable fix is a **relationship established in advance** — a named attorney (and ideally a pre-signed G-28) who already knows the person's identity and can work the ERO field office directly and grab the A-number once it's assigned at booking — rather than depending on family guessing the spelling ICE used.

### The design fork this creates

The honest complication: a G-28 names a *specific* attorney. That splits the user base.

- **Person already has a trusted lawyer** → the right artifact is a **pre-signed, undated G-28** naming that lawyer, executed now, original to the lawyer and a copy in the binder. The wallet card just records "I have a lawyer, here's who, a signed G-28 exists." This is the strong fix — and for TCB Law's own clients, you can execute these at intake and solve your own problem directly.
- **Person has no lawyer yet** (most of a free public audience) → you can't pre-sign a G-28. The card's job there is to (a) record trusted contacts, (b) record the A-number / exact legal name so family can locate them on ODLS, and (c) carry a short written **authorization for a named trusted person to receive information and retain counsel on the person's behalf** — the bridge that lets family hire you (or anyone) fast, after which the real G-28 gets signed through normal jail legal-visit channels.

This authorization-to-retain-counsel is *not* the financial/family POA you're separately building, and not the G-28 itself — it's a narrow, third instrument whose only job is "let this trusted person get me a lawyer and let that lawyer get information."

### What the contact block on the card can and can't do

- It **can**: name a trusted attorney and trusted contacts; carry the A-number and exact legal name for ODLS; reference a pre-signed G-28 if one exists; authorize a named person to retain counsel.
- It **cannot**: force ICE to disclose anything absent a G-28; substitute for the attorney actually filing G-28/EOIR-28; guarantee the lawyer is reachable or will take the case.

### Privacy note

This card concentrates sensitive data — A-number, immigration status, whether the person fears return, attorney and family contacts. Lost or seized, that's a real exposure. Worth a deliberate choice about how much goes on the *carried* card versus the binder copy, and a plain-language warning.

## Open questions for you

_Decisions already made: ship for the current worst-case situation even though it's the lesser of two evils; explain everything plainly, no overselling; open-source and free; the card carries trusted-contact and lawyer fields; this is fully separate from the family/financial POA. Same EN/ES; content is federal, so no MS/TN split — cheaper to ship than the POA work._

1. **The no-lawyer fork (the real one).** For the large share of users with no attorney yet, do we (a) keep the contact block to plain info + ODLS data and route them to find counsel, (b) add the narrow authorization-to-retain-counsel instrument, or (c) generate a fillable blank G-28 alongside the card with instructions to execute it with a lawyer now? These aren't mutually exclusive. This choice drives the whole back half of the card.
2. **Form factor.** One laminated wallet card (rights script + "do not sign" + contact/lawyer block) plus a fuller binder page (the pre-made fight-vs-depart decision, A-number, authorizations)? The carried card stays minimal; the binder holds the heavier paper.
3. **How much sensitive data rides on the carried card** vs. stays in the binder, given seizure/loss risk.
4. **Accuracy/review cadence.** Dated "current as of" line + the not-legal-advice disclaimer you already use. This content moved materially in 2025 and is still moving — it needs an owner and a recheck rhythm.
